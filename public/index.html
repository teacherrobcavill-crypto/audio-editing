3 All Component 4 Audio Editing Competencies

Every practical skill examiners test in Section A (and expect you to evaluate in Section B).

1. Trimming, Fades & Strip Silence

Remove unwanted studio noise, breaths, and bleed between phrases.

  • Top & Tail: Trim start/end of regions cleanly.
  • Zero-Crossing: Cut where waveform crosses the centre line (0).
  • Linear vs. Equal-Power Crossfade: Use equal-power (curved) for uncorrelated audio to avoid a mid-fade volume dip; linear for phase-locked material.

2. Multi-Take Comping

Assemble a "composite" master performance from multiple recorded takes.

  • Select best phrasing, tuning, and energy per line/bar.
  • Place edit boundaries in natural gaps/breaths rather than mid-vowel.
  • Match clip gain and apply short crossfades across every splice.

3. Time Correction & Quantisation

Fix rushing/dragging live performances or lock loops to project tempo.

  • Transient Slicing: Split at drum hits, quantise to grid, and fill gaps with crossfades.
  • Flex Time / Elastic Audio: Move transient markers non-destructively. Choose the right algorithm (Rhythmic/Slicing for drums, Monophonic for bass/vocals, Polyphonic for chords).
  • Artifacts to avoid: Pre-echo, flamming, metallic smearing from extreme stretching.

4. Pitch Correction & Formants

Correct intonation or create deliberate vocal effects (Melodyne / VariAudio / Flex Pitch).

  • Pitch Centre: Pulls average note pitch toward equal temperament.
  • Pitch Drift / Vibrato: Preserves natural expressiveness (avoid 0% drift unless hard-tuned pop effect is wanted).
  • Formant Shift: Keep formants preserved during corrective tuning to avoid unnatural "chipmunk" or "monster" timbre.

5. Phase Alignment & Polarity

Fix hollow, thin sound on multi-mic recordings (drums, acoustic guitar, DI + amp).

  • Polarity Invert (Ø / 180°): Flips positive/negative voltage—instant fix for opposite-facing mics (e.g., snare top vs. snare bottom).
  • Sample-Accurate Nudging: Aligns waveforms when mics are at different distances (e.g., overheads vs. close mics, or DI vs. mic'd cab), eliminating comb filtering.

6. Creative Editing & Gain Staging

Use editing as a compositional and dynamic tool (AoS 1).

  • Clip / Region Gain: Level out loud/quiet words before compression so the compressor works evenly.
  • Reverse Audio: Reverse cymbal crashes or reverb tails into downbeats.
  • Stutter / Chopping: Duplicate 1/16th or 1/32nd slices rhythmically; resample and pitch-shift.

4 Underpinning Theory: AoS 2 & AoS 3

Component 4 connects practical DAW editing to digital audio principles (AoS 2) and historical tape techniques (AoS 3).

Area of Study Core Concept What You Must Explain in the Exam
AoS 1 Non-Destructive vs. Destructive Editing DAW region edits (trims, fades, Flex markers) are non-destructive—they store edit pointers/metadata without altering the source WAV on disk. Operations like Bounce in Place, Normalise (in an audio file editor), or exporting a mix are destructive / permanent.
AoS 2 Sample Rate & Bit Depth Higher sample rates (e.g. 48 kHz / 96 kHz) give finer time resolution for sample-accurate phase nudging and cleaner time-stretching. Higher bit depth (24-bit vs. 16-bit) gives a lower noise floor (~144 dB dynamic range) when boosting quiet regions via clip gain; apply dither when bouncing down to 16-bit.
AoS 2 Varispeed vs. Time-Stretching Varispeed changes playback speed and pitch together (like speeding up tape: +100% speed = +1 octave). Modern time-stretching decouples time and pitch using granular/FFT/phase-vocoder algorithms.
AoS 3 Tape Splicing → DAW Editing Before DAWs, engineers edited 1/4" or 2" magnetic tape destructively using a chinagraph pencil to mark transients, a splicing block (45° or 90° groove), a razor blade, and splicing tape. A 45° diagonal cut acted as a physical crossfade to prevent clicks.

5 Edexcel Mark-Scheme Terminology Reference

Use precise technical terms—vague descriptions like "make it sound smoother" or "fix the timing" lose AO1/AO3 marks.

Term Exam-Ready Definition Typical Component 4 Application
Zero-Crossing Point The point where an audio waveform's amplitude passes through 0 V. Cutting here prevents DC step discontinuities and audible clicks.
Equal-Power Crossfade A fade-out/fade-in curve (often logarithmic/S-curve) that maintains constant perceived loudness across the overlap. Joining two vocal takes during comping without a volume dip.
Comb Filtering Series of regular notches and peaks in the frequency spectrum caused by summing a signal with a slightly delayed copy of itself. Diagnosing and fixing phase misalignment between drum overheads and close mics.
Polarity Inversion (Ø) Flipping a waveform 180° (positive samples become negative and vice versa) without shifting it in time. Correcting out-of-phase snare bottom mic relative to snare top mic.
Formant Preservation Maintaining the fixed resonant frequencies of a vocal tract/instrument body when shifting fundamental pitch. Correcting vocal pitch by 2–3 semitones without unnatural timbre shifts.
Transient Detection Identifying sharp, high-amplitude onset peaks (e.g. drum hits) to place warp/flex markers. Quantising live drums or slicing a breakbeat to match project BPM.

6 Edexcel Component 4 Exam Practice

Practise answering Section A/B style questions with precise AO1–AO4 detail. Expand each mark scheme to self-assess.

Question 1 (AO1/AO2 — Comping & Click Removal) 4 marks

A student has comped a lead vocal from three takes, but audible clicks occur at the edit boundaries and the vocal dips in level between phrases. Explain how to resolve both issues in a DAW.

Show Mark Scheme
  • Move edit points to natural gaps/silences or zero-crossing points where amplitude is 0 (1).
  • Apply short crossfades (e.g. 5–20 ms) across each region boundary to eliminate step clicks (1).
  • Use an equal-power crossfade curve rather than a linear fade so perceived loudness stays constant across uncorrelated takes (1).
  • Adjust clip/region gain (or volume automation) on quieter takes to match overall level before compression (1).
Question 2 (AO2/AO3 — Multi-Mic Phase Alignment) 5 marks

A bass guitar was recorded simultaneously via a DI box and a microphone placed 60 cm in front of the amplifier cabinet. When both tracks are played together, the low end sounds thin and hollow. Explain the cause and describe two editing steps to fix it.

Show Mark Scheme
  • Cause: Acoustic propagation delay (~1.7 ms at 60 cm) causes time-of-arrival differences between the DI and mic signals (1), resulting in phase cancellation / comb filtering when summed (1).
  • Step 1 (Time Alignment): Zoom in to sample level on the waveform and nudge/delay the DI track forward (or pull the mic track earlier) so the first positive transient peaks align (2).
  • Step 2 (Polarity Check): Test 180° polarity inversion (Ø) on one channel to check whether the speaker/mic wiring was inverted, keeping whichever setting maximises low-end weight (1).
Question 3 (AO3/AO4 — Time-Stretching & Pitch Correction Evaluation) 6 marks

A candidate is asked to tighten a slightly out-of-time, flat backing vocal harmony in a 120 BPM pop track. They apply rhythmic beat-slicing to the vocal and shift pitch up by +4 semitones with formant preservation turned off. Evaluate the sonic consequences and recommend better editing choices.

Show Mark Scheme
  • Time-stretching algorithm: Rhythmic/slicing mode is designed for percussive transients and will cause audible gaps, glitches, or stuttering on sustained vocal notes (1). Switch to a Monophonic Flex/Elastic Audio algorithm and warp only out-of-time syllable onsets (1).
  • Formant preservation: Shifting +4 semitones without formant preservation shifts vocal tract resonances upward, producing an unnatural "chipmunk" timbre (1); enable formant preservation to retain natural vocal character (1).
  • Pitch drift & vibrato: Apply moderate pitch-centre correction while retaining natural vibrato/drift so the harmony blends organically (1), and verify against the lead vocal for smooth harmonic tuning (1).
